### Label the negative to defuse it (accusation audit)
- **Tags:** #labeling #accusation-audit #chris-voss #hard-conversations #wording
- **What it is:** Before saying something that might land badly, name the negative reaction up front as a label, not a denial. Don't say "I don't want to be disrespectful, but..." Say **"This is going to sound disrespectful..."**, then share the observation.
- **Why it works:** A denial ("I don't want to be rude, but...") makes people brace. The "but" cancels the disclaimer (see "Say no warmly" in social.md), and they hear the rude part anyway, now with suspicion. Labeling the negative takes away its power. You've already said the worst version, so when the actual observation comes, it usually sounds milder than expected and they relax ("that wasn't so bad"). It's Chris Voss's "accusation audit" from *Never Split the Difference*: list every negative thing they might be thinking before they can think it.
- **Example:** "This is going to sound disrespectful: I think your pricing page is scaring off your best customers." Or a full accusation audit to open a tough negotiation: "You're probably going to think I'm being greedy, and that I don't appreciate everything you've done..."
- **How I'd use it:** Feedback to a boss or client, raising a price, turning down a request, or any blunt observation. Label it, then pause briefly before the observation.
- **Watch out:** Labeling doesn't make an actually disrespectful comment OK, it just softens the delivery. Don't overdo it. One label per conversation is plenty, and constant disclaimers sound insecure.

### Just ask: "What are the chances...?" (create opportunities by asking)
- **Tags:** #asking #opportunity #courage #discount #low-pressure #wording
- **What it is:** Most opportunities never happen simply because nobody asked. Make a habit of asking for things you'd normally assume are a no, like a discount, a collaboration, or a date, because *you never know*. The wording matters: **"What are the chances...?"** frames it as a *hopeful lob* rather than a request. You're tossing a possibility out there, not asking them to grant you something.
- **Why it works:** An unasked question is a guaranteed no. Asking costs a few seconds and a little awkwardness, and the upside can be big. People say yes more often than we expect: research on asking for help (e.g. Flynn & Bohns) finds we consistently *underestimate* how willing others are. A request puts the other person on the spot to grant or refuse, while a hopeful lob just floats an idea they can pick up or let drop, which keeps it playful. They're estimating, not deciding, so it's easy to say "maybe", and a "no" doesn't feel like rejection for either side.
- **Example:** At a shop: "What are the chances you could do a better price if I take two?" Usual answer: "Hmm, let me check..." With a crush: "What are the chances we grab dinner this week?" with a smile.
- **How I'd use it:** Discounts, upgrades, favors, collabs, and asking someone out. Follow up on a "maybe" with "What would make it more likely?"
- **Watch out:** Keep it light. Tone carries it, and said too seriously it can sound sarcastic. Don't overuse it with the same person or it becomes a gimmick. If the answer is clearly no, take it gracefully (see "Neediness makes you cheaper" in sales.md).
