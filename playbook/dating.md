### Frame flip: make them qualify to you (push-pull, from *The Game*)
- **Tags:** #flirting #frame #push-pull #qualification #neil-strauss
- **What it is:** Flip who is chasing whom. Mix a compliment ("pull") with a playful disqualification ("push"), so the other person ends up arguing *why they're a match for you*. It comes from Neil Strauss's *The Game*, part of the pickup-artist toolkit that also includes "negging."
- **Why it works:** People value what they have to earn (scarcity, effort justification). Once someone argues for a position ("I *am* a homebody"), they start to believe it and commit to it (consistency principle). The push-pull creates emotional ups and downs, which reads as tension and chemistry. It's the dating version of "neediness makes you cheaper": whoever seems to need it less holds the frame.
- **Example:** On a live podcast, the host says the guest is "the opposite of her type" and that she wants honesty. He says: "I'm attracted to you, but I'd never date you seriously. I need a homebody." She protests that she *is* a homebody. He keeps doubting her, and she keeps convincing him, until he reveals what he did. She laughs once she realizes she's been Neil Strauss'd.
- **How I'd use it:** The healthy version is having standards and saying them playfully: "You seem fun, but are you actually into X?" Both people end up showing whether they're a good fit, which is the same idea as qualifying in sales (see the Chris Do entry in sales.md).
- **Watch out:** It worked here because it was playful, public, and revealed right away, so she was in on the joke. Used for real, it's manipulation. It works by making someone feel slightly *not enough*, and outright negging can damage self-esteem. Many people now recognize the script, and it often ends things once spotted. It also attracts people who chase approval, which isn't a great basis for a relationship.

### 8 deep questions for a date (go past small talk)
- **Tags:** #questions #connection #self-disclosure #first-date #conversation
- **What it is:** A set of questions that skip "so what do you do?" and get to who the person really is:
  1. What's something you've changed your mind about in the last few years?
  2. What does your perfect *ordinary* day look like?
  3. What's something you're really protective of in your life?
  4. What brings out the most playful side of you?
  5. What's the biggest risk you've ever taken, and would you do it again?
  6. What's a core value or belief that guides your decisions every day?
  7. What's something you're currently working on improving about yourself?
  8. What does meaningful connection look like to you?
- **Why it works:** Closeness grows through gradual, *mutual* self-disclosure, the idea behind Arthur Aron's well-known "36 questions to fall in love" study, where strangers who took turns answering increasingly personal questions felt much closer afterward. These questions are open-ended, positive, and about values rather than résumés. They show how someone thinks (1, 6), what they care about (3, 8), and what they're like day to day (2, 4), which tells you far more about compatibility than job titles do. Asking them also signals that you're curious and emotionally mature.
- **Example:** Over dinner, after the light chat: "Okay, random one: what does your perfect *ordinary* day look like? Not a holiday, just a normal Tuesday." Then share yours too.
- **How I'd use it:** Pick 2 or 3 per date, not all 8. Use the lighter ones (2, 4) early and save the heavier ones (3, 5, 6, 8) for when things feel comfortable. Have my own answers ready, since they'll likely flip it back. Use looping ("So freedom matters more to you than security, did I get that right?") to go deeper on their answer.
- **Watch out:** Firing them off in a row feels like a job interview or a therapy session. Mix them with fun, lighthearted moments, and always share your own answer so it's an exchange, not an interrogation. Read the vibe: on a casual first coffee, question 8 might be too heavy.

### Texting: swap generic check-ins for specific ones
- **Tags:** #texting #flirting #specificity #callbacks #psychologytexting
- **What it is:** Don't send the same texts every other guy in her DMs sends: "good morning", "how are you?", "have you eaten?", "how was work?", "good night", "did you sleep well?". Send something that could only be meant for *her*: a callback to something she said, something you saw that reminded you of her, or a specific question about her day.
- **Why it works:** Generic check-ins are easy to answer on autopilot ("good, u?") and blend in with everyone else's. A specific text proves you listened and remembered (same idea as looping and the boring question audit), gives her something real to reply to, and stands out because nobody else can send it.
- **Why the post's examples feel cringe:** The post's lines ("Morning, trouble", "How's my favourite distraction doing?", "New day, same fine girl") are just as generic. They're copy-paste pick-up lines with a pet name attached, so they're still not *about her*. They also assume a level of intimacy you probably don't have yet, which reads as trying too hard. What works is specificity, not cheesiness.
- **Better examples (natural, not cheesy):**
  - Instead of *"Good morning"*: "Did you survive the 8am meeting you were dreading?" / "Saw a cat sleeping on a Grab bike this morning and thought of your cat"
  - Instead of *"How are you?"*: "Okay, how did the presentation go? Did your boss finally say something nice?"
  - Instead of *"Have you eaten?"*: "Went to that nasi lemak place you swore by. Verdict: you were right, sambal is dangerous"
  - Instead of *"How was work?"*: "Rate today out of 10. Mine's a 6, the build broke twice"
  - Instead of *"Good night"*: "Okay going to sleep, but I need your answer tomorrow: is pineapple on pizza a crime?"
  - Instead of *"Did you sleep well?"*: "Did you end up finishing that series at 3am or did sleep win?"
- **How I'd use it:** Keep a mental note of what she mentions (plans, pets, food opinions, stress at work) and text a callback later. Share a tiny piece of my own day so it's an exchange, not an interview. A playful either/or question gives her an easy, fun reply.
- **Watch out:** Don't force it. A plain "good morning" is fine once you're closer, since it's the *only-generic* pattern that's dull. Don't over-text or double-text if she's slow to reply (see "Neediness makes you cheaper"). Callbacks only work if you actually listened, so don't fake details.
