### Price anchor (the "nobody's supposed to buy this" tier)
- **Tags:** #pricing #anchoring #offer #hormozi
- **What it is:** Put a very expensive option at the top of your offer, one you don't really expect people to buy. Its job is to make your main offer look reasonable next to it.
- **Why it works:** Anchoring. People judge price by comparison, not on its own, and the first or highest number they see sets the frame. After a $10k option, a $2k option feels like a good deal, not a big spend.
- **Example:** A coaching offer with a $1,500 group program and a $15,000 1:1 "done-with-you" tier. The well-known retail case is Williams-Sonoma: adding a pricier bread maker reportedly made the cheaper one sell much better.
- **How I'd use it:** Always show at least three tiers, with the anchor shown first or most prominently. Make the anchor a real, deliverable offer.
- **Watch out:** The anchor has to be believable and something you could actually deliver. A fake one cheapens the whole page. Bonus: a few people *will* buy it, and those sales are pure upside.

### Sell to the rich first, then the masses (avoid the middle)
- **Tags:** #pricing #positioning #market #strategy #hormozi
- **What it is:** Start by selling a premium offer to people with money. Once you've built cash and scale, you can go mass market with a cheap, high-volume offer. Sitting in the middle, neither premium nor cheapest, is where businesses die.
- **Why it works:** Rich customers give high margins, so a small business can survive on few customers, invest in delivery, and fund growth. Serving the mass market profitably needs scale, systems, and capital you don't have yet. The middle gets squeezed: premium players beat you on quality and cheap players beat you on price. This matches Michael Porter's classic "stuck in the middle" warning about competitive strategy.
- **Example:** Tesla's public "master plan" went Roadster (expensive, few), then Model S, then Model 3 (mass market), using each stage's profits to fund the next.
- **How I'd use it:** For a new offer, ask "who would pay 10x for this?" and build for them first. Only cut price once there's capital and systems to win on volume.
- **Watch out:** Selling premium means delivering premium. Rich buyers expect results and service. Also, "the middle" is relative to your market, so pick a clear side instead of avoiding mid-range pricing on autopilot.

### Talk price early to qualify (money, decision maker, fit)
- **Tags:** #qualifying #discovery #sales-call #pricing #chris-do
- **What it is:** Bring up price early in a sales call to filter out prospects who can't afford you or whose buying process is a mess. Good discovery qualifies three things:
  1. **Money:** can they afford it?
  2. **Decision maker:** who actually signs off?
  3. **Fit:** do their needs match what you offer?
- **Why it works:** Money is the fastest disqualifier, so asking early avoids a 45-minute pitch to someone who can't pay. Decision complexity is a warning sign: if a simple job needs six people to approve it, the project will likely be slow and painful. Saying the price confidently and early also signals that you're the expert choosing your clients, not chasing them. It's a lighter version of the classic BANT framework (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline).
- **Example:** "Before we go deep, our projects typically start at $X. Is that in the range you were expecting? And besides you, who else is involved in making this decision?"
- **How I'd use it:** Put the three questions in the first 10 minutes of every discovery call. If two of the three fail, end politely and refer them elsewhere.
- **Watch out:** Ask with curiosity, not as an interrogation. Talking price early works best when you have enough demand to be selective. When you're desperate for work, it's tempting to skip, which is exactly when you need it most.

### Neediness makes you cheaper ("I'm good with you, I'm good without you")
- **Tags:** #mindset #pricing #objection-handling #negotiation #chris-do
- **What it is:** The moment a client feels how badly you need the job, the power shifts. You start discounting, over-explaining, chasing, and trying to force the deal, which makes them want you less. The energy to carry instead is "I'm good with you, I'm good without you." That isn't arrogance. It's believing your value doesn't disappear because someone says no.
- **Why it works:** Desperation signals low demand, and people judge value partly by how in-demand something seems (scarcity, social proof). Being willing to walk away is also the core of negotiating power: whoever needs the deal less has the stronger position (in negotiation terms, your BATNA, your best alternative if no deal happens).
- **Example:** Client: "Why so expensive?" You, calmly, without justifying or discounting: "That's the best price you'll get from me." Then stop talking and let them decide.
- **How I'd use it:** Don't drop the price at the first pushback. Keep a full pipeline so no single deal feels make-or-break. Go into calls with the frame "they also need to earn access to me."
- **Watch out:** This only works if there's real value behind it. Without the skills or demand to back it up, it comes off as bluffing. Stay warm, since detached doesn't mean cold. It pairs with qualifying early (see the Chris Do entry above).

### After the pitch: "What did you like best?" plus the conditional close
- **Tags:** #closing #questions #pitch #trial-close
- **What it is:** Two question swaps for the end of a pitch.
  1. Don't ask "Are you interested?" Ask **"What did you like best about it?"**
  2. In the conditional close, don't say **"If I can do A, B and C, would you be interested?"** Say **"If A, B and C can be done, would you be interested?"**
- **Why it works:** "Are you interested?" is a yes/no question that makes a "no" easy and puts them on the defensive, which is the kiss of death. "What did you like best?" assumes they liked something, and it gets them to *sell the idea back to you* in their own words. People believe what they hear themselves say (self-persuasion, consistency), the same effect as the 2 Lego technique. In the close, "If *I* can do this" puts you above the client as the hero with the power, and quietly implies they can't. "If this *can be done*" is neutral and collaborative, so it sounds like a problem you'll figure out together. Either way, a conditional close sets the terms of a yes and brings out the real objection: if they say no, you learn exactly what's missing.
- **Example:** "So, what stood out to you most?" They answer: "The automated reporting, honestly." "Great. If reporting can be live in 30 days, with your team trained and within budget, would you be interested in moving forward?"
- **How I'd use it:** Swap these in at the end of every pitch or demo. Build A, B and C from what they said they liked best, plus anything they raised during discovery (see the qualifying entry above).
- **Watch out:** Only list conditions you can actually deliver, since the yes is tied to them. If they can't name anything they liked, that's a real signal, so don't push past it.
