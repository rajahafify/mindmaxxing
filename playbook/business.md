### The three roles every company needs
- **Tags:** #team #hiring #startup #hormozi
- **What it is:** A business only needs three roles covered: a **Promoter** who sells the product, a **Builder** who makes the product, and an **Operator** who runs the day to day. One person can cover all three, or each role can be a team of 20.
- **Why it works:** It turns "who should I hire?" into "which role is uncovered?" Most stalled businesses are missing one of the three, like a great product nobody sells, or strong sales with delivery falling apart.
- **Example:** A solo founder starts as all three. The first hire is usually whichever role the founder is weakest at or hates most, often operations.
- **How I'd use it:** Audit any business or partnership by asking "who's the promoter, builder, and operator?" When picking co-founders, look for someone strong in a role I'm not.
- **Watch out:** One person holding all three roles can do it early on but burns out at scale. Overlap matters too: two builders and no promoter is a common startup failure.

### Signal vs. noise: 3 things in the next 18 hours
- **Tags:** #productivity #focus #priorities #kevin-oleary #steve-jobs
- **What it is:** Each day, pick the 3 things you must get done in the next 18 hours. That's the *signal*. Everything else, like emails, meetings, and busywork, is *noise*. Kevin O'Leary tells this as a lesson he got from Steve Jobs.
- **Why it works:** Having too many priorities means having none. A short, hard limit forces you to decide what actually moves the needle, instead of reacting to whatever is loudest. 18 hours is roughly one waking day plus the evening before, short enough to be urgent but long enough to finish real work. Three is a small enough number to keep in your head.
- **Example:** Tonight, write: (1) send proposal to Client X, (2) record this week's video, (3) fix the checkout bug. Tomorrow, do those before checking anything else. Anything that doesn't serve them waits.
- **How I'd use it:** Write the 3 the night before or first thing in the morning, then review at the end of the day. Ask about any new task, "is this signal or noise?"
- **Watch out:** Pick outcomes, not activities ("send the proposal", not "work on the proposal"). Some noise, like admin and replies, still needs a batch time slot so it doesn't pile up. And don't let urgent-but-unimportant tasks sneak into the top 3.

### Proximity is power
- **Tags:** #networking #mentorship #environment #growth #tony-robbins
- **What it is:** Get physically and socially close to the people who are already where you want to be: their rooms, events, teams, and conversations. Being near them gives you access to opportunities, knowledge, and standards you can't get from a distance. Tony Robbins's line is "proximity is power."
- **Why it works:** Most opportunities travel through relationships, so whoever is in the room hears about them first. You also absorb a group's norms. What feels "normal" (ambition, work ethic, deal size) adjusts to the people around you, the idea behind Jim Rohn's "you're the average of the five people you spend the most time with." And watching people up close teaches the unwritten details that books and reels leave out.
- **Example:** Instead of only watching game-dev content online, volunteer at a big KL game dev event, help out a studio founder you admire, or join a publisher's demo day. One conversation in that room can do more than a month of cold emails.
- **How I'd use it:** Each month, pick one room to get into that's a level above where I am now. Offer to help, not just to network (see "Talk to strangers" in social.md). Check who I spend most of my time with and whether they pull me up or hold me back.
- **Watch out:** Show up with value, not just to take. People in the room can tell who's only there to extract. Being in the room isn't the same as contributing, so you still have to do the work. Don't cut off old friends just because they're not "high level". Add people, don't trade them in.
- **Source:** Tony Robbins (no link, shared from memory), noted 2026-10-28

### Ask for intros with a forwardable email
- **Tags:** #networking #introductions #asking #low-friction #nickjayala
- **What it is:** Don't ask a contact a vague "Can you introduce me to X?" Ask: **"Would you be open to forwarding something to him? I wrote it so you can just hit forward."** Then attach a short, three-sentence note they'd feel proud and comfortable sending for you.
- **Why it works:** "Can you introduce me?" quietly hands your contact a writing task. They have to explain who you are, why it matters, and what you want, so it sits in their inbox and dies. A ready-to-forward note brings their effort down to one click, and lower friction means more yeses. It also controls the message, so you're introduced exactly how you want, and it protects their reputation: a short, clear, respectful note makes *them* look good for sending it. It also lets the recipient say yes or no without awkwardness (the "double opt-in" norm).
- **Example (forwardable note):**
  > Hi Daniel, I'm Hafify, founder of a new indie game studio in KL; before that I spent 12 years as a software engineer at places like AirAsia. We're building a cozy co-op game and are looking for a publisher with SEA reach, and Aiman mentioned you know that space better than anyone. Would you be open to a 20-minute call in the next couple of weeks?
- **How I'd use it:** Any warm intro: publishers, investors, potential hires, mentors. Keep it to three sentences: **who I am (with credibility) → what I'm doing and why them → one small, specific ask.** Make the subject line forward-friendly ("Intro: Hafify (indie studio, KL) ↔ Daniel").
- **Watch out:** Keep it short and humble. Your contact is lending you their name, so nothing salesy or pushy. Make the ask small (a short call, not "fund my game"). Give them an easy out ("totally fine if it's not a fit"). If they forward it, thank them and update them on what happened.
